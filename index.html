About Me

I am a doctoral candidate in the Economics and Planning Unit at the Indian Statistical Institute, Delhi Centre. My research interests lie at the intersection of Human Capital, Development Macroeconomics, the Economics of Education, Labour Economics, and the Rural Economy in India.

My doctoral thesis comprises three chapters on tertiary and foundational education in India. a) Transforming Rural Economies through Tertiary Education: Evidence from India. This chapter examines how college-educated individuals residing in rural India contribute to rural prosperity and, more broadly, to structural transformation. b) Foundational Skills and Labor Market Outcomes: Evidence from India. Here I ask whether a program that raised foundational learning in primary school also raised earnings in early adulthood, following the same birth cohorts from childhood test scores to young-adult labor market outcomes. c) Relaxing Entry Constraints: College Expansion and Tertiary Attainment in Rural India. The final chapter, my job market paper, asks whether the rapid expansion of colleges in Rajasthan after 2003 raised tertiary attainment among exposed cohorts, and what determined where the new colleges were established.

Methodologically, my work combines large-scale Indian administrative and survey data with applied econometric methods—including instrumental variables, regression discontinuity, and staggered difference-in-differences and event-study designs—alongside machine-learning tools for prediction.

Working Papers

Ravinder. "Relaxing Entry Constraints: College Expansion and Tertiary Attainment in Rural India." Job Market Paper.

Tertiary education attainment among young adults in India has risen sharply over the past three decades, alongside a large expansion in the number of higher education institutions. This paper asks whether expanded access to colleges raised local rural tertiary education attainment. I study Rajasthan, which follows a similar trend to India in rural tertiary attainment but has experienced the highest rate of college expansion among Indian states. I combine geocoded college locations from the All India Survey on Higher Education with unique cohort-level records from the Socio-Economic and Caste Census 2011. Using individuals aged 22 to 31 in 2011, I construct a village-by-birth-cohort panel spanning 1998 to 2007, linking each cohort to the college supply prevailing when it reached college-entry age. The expansion began in 2003, and I exploit the staggered entry of colleges across villages. Exposure to a college within 15 km raised the tertiary attainment of exposed cohorts by about 1.4 percentage points two years after exposure and by 2.2-2.4 percentage points after four to five years, with no evidence of pre-trends. The effect is robust to several checks, including alternative comparison groups, distance thresholds, restricted cohort window, and adjustments for local economic activity, and holds for both men and women. Turning to college placement, district land fractionalization in 1999 is strongly associated with subsequent college growth. More- and less-fractionalized districts had similar college stocks before the expansion but diverged only after it began. This pattern is consistent with fragmented, lower-value agricultural land offering cheaper sites for a land-intensive, largely private expansion of higher education.

Ravinder. "Foundational Skills and Labor Market Outcomes: Evidence from India." Working Paper, 2025.

The expansion of primary schooling in developing countries has not been matched by gains in learning. This paper asks whether a program that raised foundational learning in primary school also raised earnings in early adulthood. I exploit the eligibility rule of India’s District Primary Education Programme (DPEP), which targeted districts whose 1991 female literacy rate fell below the national average, in a regression discontinuity design. I observe the same birth cohorts in rural India at two stages of life: as children aged 8-11 in the Annual Status of Education Report from 2006 to 2011, and as young adults aged 22-25 in the Periodic Labour Force Survey from 2020 to 2025. DPEP eligibility raised test scores by 0.14 standard deviations, with similar gains for boys and girls. As young adults, the same cohorts earn about 32 percent more per week in regular wage or salaried jobs, but I find no earnings gains among the self-employed or casual laborers. The earnings gain is positive and significant for men but not for women. The results are robust to a range of checks. More broadly, the findings suggest that the returns to education in developing countries depend not only on how long children stay in school but also, importantly, on what they learn there.

Castelló-Climent, Amparo, Abhiroop Mukhopadhyay, and Ravinder. "Transforming Rural Economies through Tertiary Education: Evidence from India." WIDER Working Paper No. 2023/89, 2023.

This paper examines whether tertiary-educated individuals who live in rural India contribute to local prosperity. Using village-level data, we estimate the causal impact of tertiary education on rural development, exploiting the location of Catholic missions circa 1911, historically associated with the expansion of higher education, as an instrumental variable. A one percentage point increase in the village share of tertiary-educated individuals raises per capita consumption by about 7.2 percent, a result robust to using night-light density as an alternative prosperity measure and to a wide range of further checks. We provide evidence consistent with two mechanisms: a conventional channel, in which tertiary-educated individuals access productive private-sector employment locally and in nearby towns, and an agricultural channel, in which households with a tertiary-educated member show higher agricultural productivity, greater access to technical advice, and more crop diversification. The findings point to a distinct form of structural transformation occurring within rural areas, alongside that driven by migration to cities.
